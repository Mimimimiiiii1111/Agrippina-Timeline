Agrippina the Younger

(AD 15–59)

Birth and family

Date: 6 November AD 15

Agrippina the Younger was born in Ara Ubiorum, which was a Roman settlement in Germania Inferior. It's near modern-day Cologne, Germany. She belonged to the Julio-Claudian dynasty, Rome's first imperial family.

Learn more

Her father was Germanicus, a popular Roman general. Her mother, Agrippina the Elder, was a granddaughter of Augustus, Rome's first emperor. She was the sister of Caligula, who became emperor in AD 37.

Sources: [1], [2], [9]

Family history

Date: AD 19–33

Agrippina grew up during a period of political conflict within the imperial family. Her father died when she was young, and later her mom and older brothers were persecuted by Emperor Tiberius.

Learn more

Germanicus died in AD 19 at Antioch. Agrippina the Elder was exiled and died in AD 33. Her brothers Nero Caesar and Drusus Caesar also died after being imprisoned.

Sources: [1], [2], [3]

Marriage and her son Nero

Date: AD 28–37

Agrippina married Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus and had a son, Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus, who was later known as Emperor Nero.

Learn more

Agrippina was around 13 when the marriage took place. Nero was born on December 15, AD 37. Nero's future would become a major focus of her political ambitions.

Sources: [1], [2], [3]

Exile under Caligula

Date: AD 37–41

When her brother Caligula became emperor, Agrippina at first was a prominent member of the imperial family. In AD 39, she and her sister Julia Livilla were exiled amid accusations of conspiracy.

Learn more

Caligula ruled from AD 37 to 41. Ancient accounts connect the accusations to a suspected conspiracy involving Marcus Aemilius Lepidus. Claudius allowed Agrippina to return after Caligula's assassination in AD 41.

Sources: [1], [3]

Marriage to Emperor Claudius

Date: AD 49

Agrippina married Emperor Claudius, her maternal uncle, in AD 49. Their marriage was very controversial and was even brought up in the Roman Senate.

Learn more

Roman law at the time did not allow marriage between an uncle and his niece. The Senate approved a legal change to permit their marriage. Agrippina became empress and gained access to the imperial court.

Sources: [1], [2], [3]

The title Augusta and Nero's Adoption

Date: AD 50

Agrippina received the title Augusta and convinced Claudius to adopt Nero, which placed her son in a stronger position to become emperor.

Learn more

Augusta was an important imperial title. Claudius already had a biological son, Britannicus. Nero became Claudius's adopted son and a candidate for becoming emperor.

Sources: [1], [2], [3], [6]

Preparing Nero to Rule

Date: Early 50s AD

Agrippina helped build Nero's future by having him educated by different advisers. One was the philosopher Seneca, whom she helped return from exile. She also supported Burrus's appointment to strengthen Nero's future.

Learn more

Seneca became one of Nero's tutors. Sextus Afranius Burrus became commander of the Praetorian Guard. Marcus Antonius Pallas was another court figure associated with Agrippina.

Sources: [2], [3], [8]

Claudius Dies and Nero Becomes Emperor

Date: October 13, AD 54

Claudius died in AD 54, and Nero became emperor. Agrippina held a lot of influence during Nero's early reign.

Learn more

Ancient historian Tacitus claimed that Claudius was poisoned. Some ancient accounts suggest Agrippina arranged the poisoning, but exactly how Claudius died is uncertain.

Sources: [3], [7]

Power during Nero's Reign

Date: AD 54–55

During Nero's early reign, Agrippina appeared in public alongside her son a lot. She had an unusual amount of political influence for a Roman woman during that time period.

Learn more

She received public honors and was pictured on imperial coins. She had connections with senators, military leaders, and imperial advisers. She had influence without holding an official magistracy.

Sources: [2], [5], [6]

Relationship with Nero

Date: AD 55–59

Nero wanted to have independence from his mom's influence. As Agrippina lost political support, her relationship with Nero worsened and became a threat to her position.

Learn more

Nero's advisers and allies changed over time. Agrippina did not have as much influence during his reign. Ancient historians describe a struggle for control of the imperial court.

Sources: [2], [4], [5]

Agrippina's Death

Date: AD 59

Agrippina was killed near Baiae in AD 59, according to ancient accounts, on Nero's orders. Ancient accounts mention an earlier failed attempt involving an arranged boat.

Learn more

Tacitus writes about the attempted boat incident in dramatic detail. The details of her death come mainly from ancient accounts written after her lifetime.

Sources: [2], [4]

Agrippina's Legacy

Date: After AD 59

Agrippina was a big part of Roman political history. During her life she built up a lot of influence as a woman in the imperial court.

Learn more

She was represented on coins and in portrait sculpture. The settlement where she was born later became the Roman colony Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium. Ancient writers often portrayed her as manipulative and dangerous.

Sources: [2], [5], [9], [10], [11]
